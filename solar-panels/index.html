Portable Solar Panels

A 100-watt panel almost never makes 100 watts. Heat, angle, shade and wiring all take a share before the power reaches your battery. Know how much a panel really makes and you will buy the right number of them the first time.

See the setups

What a portable panel does, and what it does not

A portable solar panel charges a battery. It does not power your appliances on its own. The panel feeds a power station, and the power station runs your fridge, lights and phone.

That is why the panel question always comes second. First you size the battery for what you use. Then you size the panels to refill it.

Jackery calls a power station plus panels a solar generator. If you have not picked a power station yet, start with our best solar generator guide.

How much a panel really makes

A panel's rating comes from a lab test at 25°C (77°F) under bright, direct light. In the field, plan on about 75 percent of that rating. Then multiply by your peak sun hours, which is the number of hours of full-strength sun your area gets in a day.

Daily watt-hours = panel watts × peak sun hours × 0.75

Most of the US gets about 4 to 5.5 peak sun hours a day. Our off-grid guide lists them by region.

100 Wabout 340 Whabout 410 Wh
200 Wabout 675 Whabout 825 Wh
400 W (two 200 W panels)about 1,350 Whabout 1,650 Wh

So a 100-watt panel covers a phone, lights and a laptop with room to spare. It takes roughly 200 watts to keep up with a 12-volt camping fridge, and about 400 watts to keep up with a full-size home fridge.

Four things that cut a panel's output

Heat

Panels make less power as they get hotter. Most lose between 0.2 and 0.5 percent of output for every degree Celsius above their 25°C rating, and cells in full summer sun can reach about 65°C. That is a loss of roughly 8 to 20 percent on a hot day. Set panels where air can move behind them, not flat on hot pavement.

Angle

In the US, face the panel south and tilt it at about your latitude. Tilt it 10 to 15 degrees flatter in summer and 10 to 15 degrees steeper in winter. A portable panel has one big advantage over a rooftop one: you can turn it to follow the sun every couple of hours.

Shade

Even a shadow across part of a panel can cut its output far more than the shaded area suggests. A branch, a pole or the edge of an awning matters. Move the panel into fully open sun.

Clouds and glass

Heavy overcast cuts output to a small part of the rating. A panel propped behind a window also loses a lot through the glass. Put it outside, in the open.

Matching panels to your power station

Every power station has a solar input limit, the most panel watts it can use. Panels beyond that limit charge nothing extra. Check that number before you buy panels.

For other models, the solar input limit is listed on the product page. Also confirm the panel's plug fits your station's solar input before you buy, especially when mixing brands.

Bifacial panels

Several current SolarSaga panels are bifacial, meaning the back side also catches light reflected off the ground. They gain the most over light surfaces such as sand, concrete or snow, and less over dark grass or dirt.

What portable panels will not do

They will not run appliances without a battery in between. They will not make their full rating on a hot afternoon, under clouds or in partial shade. And no reasonable number of portable panels will power a whole house. For that, look at a permanent rooftop system from a licensed installer.

Common questions

How much does a 100-watt panel make in a day?

At 4.5 peak sun hours, roughly 340 Wh after real-world losses. In strong desert sun it can reach 400 to 500 Wh.

How many panels do I need for a 2,000 Wh power station?

Refilling 2,042 Wh in one day at 4.5 sun hours takes about 600 watts of panel. The Explorer 2000 v2 accepts up to 400 watts, so two 200-watt panels refill it in about a day and a half of good sun.

Do they work on cloudy days?

They still make some power, but much less. Size your battery for cloudy stretches instead of counting on panels alone.

Can I use another brand's panels with a Jackery?

Sometimes, if the panel's voltage falls inside the station's solar input range and you have the right adapter. Check both specs first. Matching panels from the same maker is the simplest path.
