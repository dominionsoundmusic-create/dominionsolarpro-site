Off Grid Solar Power

A portable solar setup can run a cabin, a shed or a tiny home's essentials. It cannot run an average American house. Here is the math that tells you which side of that line you are on, and what to buy.

See the setups

The honest answer, first

The average US home bought about 10,791 kWh of electricity in 2022, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. That works out to roughly 30 kWh a day.

The biggest single Jackery unit, the Explorer 5000 Plus, holds 5.04 kWh. Carrying an average house for one day would take six of them, plus close to 9,000 watts of panels to refill them every day. That is no longer a portable setup. That is an installed solar system with a battery bank, and you would want a licensed installer.

Where portable off grid solar power shines is everywhere smaller than that:

  • A hunting cabin, fishing camp or weekend place
  • A shed, workshop or barn with no power run to it
  • A tiny home that heats and cooks with propane or wood
  • The essentials of a full house during a long outage

If one of those is you, the rest of this guide will get you to a specific, buyable setup.

Count your watt-hours

Everything starts with one number: how many watt-hours (Wh) you use in 24 hours. A watt is how hard something pulls at one moment. A watt-hour is that pull kept up for an hour. A 50-watt fan running 8 hours uses 400 Wh.

These are typical figures for common off-grid loads. Your own equipment will differ, so check each label.

The yellow label trick. Every refrigerator and freezer sold in the US carries a yellow EnergyGuide label with an estimated yearly kWh figure. Divide it by 365 and multiply by 1,000. A fridge rated at 450 kWh a year uses about 1,230 Wh a day. That one number is usually the biggest steady load in an off-grid kitchen.

Size it with two rules

Battery: two days of your daily use. Off grid there is no campground hookup or truck alternator to fall back on. Two days of storage carries you through a cloudy stretch.

Panels: daily use, divided by your peak sun hours, divided by 0.75. A peak sun hour is one hour of full-strength sunlight. Most of the country gets between 4 and 5.5 of them a day. The 0.75 covers heat, dust, angle and wiring losses.

Where you live changes the answer a lot. These are average daily peak sun hours from National Renewable Energy Laboratory data:

RegionPeak sun hours a day

Run a 3,000 Wh cabin through both rules. The battery needs 6,000 Wh. In Arizona at 5.5 sun hours, you need about 730 watts of panels. In western Oregon at 3.5 sun hours, you need about 1,150 watts for the same cabin. That is close to 60 percent more panel for the same fridge and the same lights.

These are yearly averages. Winter days are shorter and the sun sits lower, so if you will use the place in December, size your panels for December.

Four things that break off-grid setups

Heating with electricity

A 1,500-watt space heater uses 1,500 Wh every hour it runs. One cold evening can empty a battery that would otherwise last two days. Heat with propane or wood and keep the solar for lights, fridge and pump.

The well pump's start-up kick

Pumps and other motors pull two to three times their running watts for a moment when they start. Check the pump's start-up rating against the power station's surge rating before you buy. Some deep-well pumps also need 240 volts, which most portable units do not supply.

Charging in freezing weather

Most lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries should not be charged below freezing. Check the manual for your unit and keep it inside the insulated part of the cabin, not out in the shed.

Leaving the inverter on

The inverter turns battery power into wall-outlet power, and it uses power just being switched on. On a big unit that can be 30 to 50 watts around the clock. If a load can run on the 12-volt or USB ports, use those, and switch AC off when nothing needs it.

When you need an installed system instead

Portable units stop making sense when your daily use runs past about 10 kWh, when you heat or cool the whole home with electricity, or when you want to live completely off grid in a standard house.

At that point you want a permanent array, a wall-mounted battery bank and a licensed installer, plus the permits your county requires. A portable unit still has a job there, as the backup that keeps the fridge and the internet running while the main system is serviced.

Common questions

How many solar panels do I need to run a house off grid?

An average US home uses close to 30 kWh a day. At 4.5 peak sun hours with about 25 percent real-world losses, that takes roughly 8,900 watts of panels, about 22 panels of 400 watts each. That is a permanent rooftop or ground-mount system, not a portable one.

Can a portable solar generator run a refrigerator off grid?

Yes. A full-size refrigerator typically uses 1,000 to 1,600 Wh a day. Use the yellow label trick above to get your own number. A 2,000 Wh unit with enough panel to refill it each day runs one comfortably.

How many days of battery do I need?

Plan on two full days of normal use. Off grid there is nothing to fall back on, and two days gets you through a cloudy stretch.

How long do the batteries last?

Jackery rates its current LiFePO4 units at 4,000 cycles to 70 percent capacity, and the HomePower 2000 Plus v2 at 6,000 or more. At one full cycle a day, Jackery estimates about 11 years and about 16 years.

Is off grid solar power the same as home backup?

The math is the same, but the goal is different. Off grid, solar is your only source. For backup, the grid comes back and you only need to bridge the outage. Our home backup guide covers that case.
