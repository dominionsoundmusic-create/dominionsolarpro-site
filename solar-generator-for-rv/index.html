Solar Generator for RV

Start with the question everyone actually has, because the honest answer changes what you buy: no, a portable solar generator will not run your rooftop air conditioner all night. Here is what it will run, how to size it, and which unit fits.

See the setups

The air conditioning answer, first

A standard 13,500 BTU rooftop RV air conditioner draws roughly 1,500 to 1,800 watts while running, and the compressor pulls two to three times that for a moment when it starts. Running it takes a unit that can absorb that start, and keeping it running takes thousands of watt-hours for every hour of cooling.

A full-time rig running rooftop AC two to three hours a day can need 10,000 to 14,000 watt-hours daily. That is not a portable power station. That is a house battery bank.

The short version. A large portable unit can run your air conditioner for an hour or so in the afternoon heat. It cannot cool your rig overnight. Plan your cooling around shade, ventilation, altitude and campground hookups, and size your solar generator for everything else.

Work out your daily watt-hours

Everything follows from what you use in 24 hours. Four realistic RV profiles, built from typical draws rather than the biggest number on the label:

ProfileWhat runsPer day
Weekend12-volt cooler, fan, phones, lightsabout 880 Wh
Weekend plus a CPAPThe above plus a CPAP overnightabout 1,330 Wh
Full-timer, no ACFridge, devices, lights, water pump, microwave in burstsabout 1,400 Wh
Class A, residential fridge, induction cooktopA full household kitchen6,500 to 9,000 Wh

Two things in that table surprise people. A 12-volt cooler is the biggest steady load in most camps: a compressor drawing 50 watts about 40 percent of the time averages 20 watts, which is 480 Wh a day. And a CPAP nearly doubles a weekend load on its own. A common machine at 56 watts uses about 450 Wh over an eight-hour night, and more with the heated humidifier on.

Then size it with two rules

Battery: 1.5 times your daily use. That carries you through a cloudy day without starting the truck. In an RV you also have shore power and alternator charging to fall back on, so 1.5 days is enough for most trips.

Panels: enough to replace one day's use in about five peak sun hours. Panels do not make their rated output in the field. Plan on about 75 percent of the number on the box, less in heat and at a poor angle. A 100-watt panel makes about 340 Wh on a typical day and 400 or more in strong desert sun.

Run the weekend-plus-CPAP profile through both rules: 1,330 Wh a day means about 2,000 Wh of battery and roughly 350 watts of panel. That is a specific, buyable answer. Our solar panel guide shows the panel math in more detail.

Three things that quietly ruin the math

Inverter idle drain

The inverter turns battery power into wall-outlet power, and it uses power just being on. A big inverter can use 30 to 50 watts doing nothing. Left on for 24 hours that is 720 to 1,200 Wh, often more than your fridge. Switch AC off when nothing needs it.

Running a 12-volt fridge through the inverter

If your fridge runs on 12 volts and your power station has a 12-volt output, use it directly. Converting DC to AC and back to DC wastes a real share of that load for nothing.

Battery chemistry

Current Jackery units use LiFePO4 batteries rated around 4,000 cycles to 70 percent capacity. Older lithium-ion packs were rated for far fewer. For weekends the difference barely matters. For full-timing it decides whether you buy once or twice.

The air conditioning question, answered with numbers

Explorer 2000 Plus runs up to 3,000 W and holds about 2,042 Wh, and Jackery says it expands from 2 to 24 kWh with add-on battery packs. With a soft-start kit installed on the air conditioner, a unit in this class can start a typical 13,500 BTU rooftop AC. Without one, the start-up surge can trip it.

The limit is energy, not power. At about 2,042 Wh against a 1,500-watt running draw, you get roughly 1.3 hours before the battery is empty. Battery packs stretch that, but you are still counting hours, not nights.

Panels for an RV

SolarSaga 100 Air folds up for travel at about 7.1 pounds. SolarSaga 200W is what you want if you run a 2,000 Wh unit down each day and need it back by evening. For panels that stay on the roof, look at the flexible SolarSaga 100 Light or the rigid SolarSaga 100 Prime.

What this setup will not do

It will not run rooftop air conditioning overnight. It will not replace a properly installed house battery bank and roof array for full-time living. It will not charge much in dense shade or heavy overcast, which is exactly when you want power most. And it will not work out if you size it by watts instead of watt-hours, the most common mistake in this category.

What it will do is run a realistic camp quietly, without fuel, without generator-hour rules, and without the upkeep a gas generator needs.

Common questions

Can I run a microwave?

In short bursts, yes, on the larger units. A 1,000-watt microwave for a few minutes uses little energy even though it pulls a lot of power.

How long will a 2,000 Wh unit last?

Divide by your real daily use. At the 1,400 Wh full-timer profile it is roughly a day and a half with nothing coming in from solar.

Do I need a 30-amp or 50-amp setup?

That describes your shore power connection, not your battery. A 30-amp rig running one rooftop AC typically wants 2,000 to 3,000 watts of inverter output. A 50-amp rig with two air conditioners is well past what portable units do.

Can I charge while driving?

Most Jackery units accept 12-volt car charging. It is slow, but a few hours on the highway makes a real difference to the next night.
